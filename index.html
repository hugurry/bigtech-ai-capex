Big Tech’s AI bill has outgrown its cash flow

In their latest quarters, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta each spent more on capital expenditure than their operations generated in cash, and all three raised new debt or equity in 2026. Microsoft’s cash capex is still well inside its operating cash flow, and it kept returning cash to shareholders.

Data as of 25 October 2026. Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft have not yet reported their third quarter. Amazon has said it reports on 29 October and Alphabet is expected on 28 October; Microsoft’s date was not checked. This page will be updated when they report.

The question: who is paying for AI with its own money?

I compared capital expenditure (capex) with operating cash flow at Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft, using each company’s own earnings releases and annual report. When capex is above 100% of operating cash flow, a company is investing more than its operations generate in cash. The difference has to come from cash reserves, new debt or new equity.

Alphabet Meta

Alphabet’s Q3 2026 has not been reported yet. Amazon and Microsoft are not drawn as lines because a quarter-by-quarter series was not collected for them; they are compared in the next two sections.

  • Alphabet: capex went from 49% of operating cash flow in Q3 2025 to 115% in Q2 2026, against 81% in Q2 2025. Quarterly capex nearly doubled, from $24.0bn to $44.9bn, while operating cash flow fell from $48.4bn to $39.1bn. Free cash flow was −$5.9bn.
  • Amazon: in Q2 2026 capex (net of proceeds and incentives) was $53.1bn against operating cash flow of $45.4bn, or 117%, compared with 96% a year earlier. Over twelve months its free cash flow is −$7.6bn, versus +$18.2bn a year before.
  • Meta: the ratio stayed between 61% and 65% for three quarters, then jumped to 98% in Q2 2026 and 106% in Q3 2026, when free cash flow was −$2.1bn.
  • Microsoft: capex was 65% of operating cash flow in April to June 2026, up from 40% a year earlier, and 63% over its fiscal year. It paid $26.4bn in dividends and bought back $22.3bn of shares in the year.

Amazon: purchases of property and equipment net of proceeds from sales and incentives, a line Amazon reports directly. How the gap is being filled

Alphabet, Amazon and Meta have all raised new borrowing in 2026, and Alphabet also sold $49.6bn of shares and preferred stock. Alphabet said the proceeds are for general corporate purposes, including capital expenditures to scale AI infrastructure. Meta has stopped buying back its own shares this year, and has approved a new class of non-voting stock that, in its own words, will help it finance growth. Microsoft issued no new debt in fiscal 2026 and repaid $3.0bn.

Why not look at profit?

Net income is a poor guide this year. Alphabet reported a net gain of $98.0bn in other income in the second quarter, mostly unrealised gains on equity securities. Amazon’s second-quarter net income includes $53.4bn of non-operating income, primarily from its investments in Anthropic, the company that develops Claude, the AI assistant used to help prepare this analysis. Microsoft’s fourth quarter includes a $3.2bn gain on its investment in Anthropic. Cash flow is not affected by those revaluations, which is why this study uses it.

What this does not show

  • Capex is not the same as AI spending. It is an upper bound: data centres and servers also serve cloud and core products, and the companies do not report an exact split.
  • Cash capex understates what has been committed. Amazon’s property and equipment acquired but not yet paid rose by $29.3bn over the twelve months. Microsoft’s cash additions exclude finance leases, its other investing outflows rose by $22.2bn, mainly to facilitate the purchase of components, and its lease obligations total $443.5bn including imputed interest. Its ratios are therefore a floor.
  • A ratio above 100% is not a warning on its own. These companies hold large cash balances (Meta had $82.45bn of cash and marketable securities at 30 September 2026; Alphabet $242.5bn at 30 June) and can borrow. Whether the spending pays off is a question about returns, which this analysis does not assess.
  • It describes what happened, not what comes next. Capex guidance for 2026 and 2027 changes every quarter, and secondary sources did not always agree on it, so guidance is left out.
  • This is not investment advice.
